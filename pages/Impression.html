Impression as a method of recognition

Travelers and naturalists of the 19 century's, although lacking photographs, knew well how to convey unfamiliar landscapes in words. When describing such a place, one must convey not only the details of the scene but also the overall impression of what one sees.

Impression is such important information about an object that a landscape that leaves no impression doesn't linger in the memory for long. Even if every detail of the painting were described with meticulous care, such a description only becomes tiresome, and the painting itself quickly begins to fade from view.

I will try to prove here that this is not some subjective feature of the individual, but a memorizing mechanism.

An impression is your reaction to a signal sent by an object's position in space. This is the reaction of any organism to environmental cues. A living organism primarily requires a signal of alarm, threat, and danger. This could be an obstacle, a hostile environment, a trap, or a predator. It could also be information from internal control sensors—a red light. Each signal from a sensor conveys a message that its information is unfavorable. The more sensitive the sensors are to the slightest deviation, the more likely they are to all emit a red alarm. Which means—Stop!

But stopping every action because of small danger signals when there is already a goal is perhaps an even worse strategy.

A positive reaction is the ability to disable some of the sensors that are giving an alarm signal, since their information is not essential for the intended purpose.

Pleasure is not a signal. It is the absence or removal of signals.

A positive response is the ability to disable some alarm sensors in order to achieve a desired goal. For example, something related to food. Enjoying food blocks some of the signals warning of danger if these warnings are not so important. The ability to reproduce blocks an even larger range of alarm signals.

But the Impression itself isn't composed solely of activated and deactivated alarm signals. Between them, there must also be a zone of low intensity. The signal isn't turned off, but rather reduced to a low level. The object attracts attention, but evokes neither fear nor pleasure. Perhaps it will be explored, perhaps simply forgotten. This depends on the current superposition in relation to the object.
